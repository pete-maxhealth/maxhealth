Section One

Understanding What You Are Dealing With

Glioblastoma (GBM) is a Grade IV primary brain tumour. It is the most aggressive form of brain cancer, and the statistics — a median survival of around 14 months with standard treatment — are genuinely difficult to read. It is important to understand them honestly.

But understanding the statistics also means understanding what drives them — and therefore where the opportunities to improve on them lie.

Understanding the Evidence: Throughout this document, evidence is drawn from different stages of research. Preclinical evidence comes from laboratory cell studies (in vitro) and animal models (in vivo) — promising but not yet proven in humans. Clinical evidence comes from human trials: Phase I establishes safety, Phase II tests for efficacy signals, Phase III involves large-scale randomised controlled trials. Where evidence is preclinical only, this is stated clearly. Where human clinical trial data exists, this is highlighted. Much of the adjuvant protocol described here is supported by early-phase clinical or strong preclinical evidence — genuinely promising, but not yet at the standard of Phase III proof. That honest distinction is important.
🔬

The Warburg Effect

GBM cells are heavily dependent on glucose for energy — far more so than normal brain cells. This metabolic vulnerability is one of the most important targets available to patients.

🧬

MGMT Methylation

Approximately 40% of GBM patients have a methylated MGMT promoter. This means chemotherapy (TMZ) works more effectively in their tumour. Ask your oncologist about your MGMT status — it is the single most important biomarker for your treatment response.

🛡️

Immune Evasion

GBM actively suppresses the immune system's ability to recognise and attack it. Supporting immune surveillance is therefore a meaningful therapeutic target alongside standard treatment.

🔄

Treatment Resistance

GBM frequently develops resistance to TMZ chemotherapy over time. Understanding and pre-empting resistance mechanisms is a key strategic priority.

A Word for Unmethylated Patients — Do Not Give Up Hope

This document was written by a patient with MGMT-methylated GBM, and several elements of the protocol — particularly the CBD-TMZ synergy and metformin benefit — have the strongest clinical evidence specifically in methylated patients. It would be easy for someone with unmethylated status to read this and feel the protocol is less relevant to them. That would be the wrong conclusion.

The vast majority of this protocol applies equally and powerfully regardless of MGMT status. The metabolic strategy — very low carb diet, intermittent fasting, GKI optimisation — targets the Warburg Effect which is present in all GBM regardless of methylation. The cannabinoids target CB1, CB2, GPR55 and TRPV1 receptors that are overexpressed in all GBM. The anti-inflammatory stack, the immune surveillance support, the exercise, the mindset, the repurposed drugs targeting angiogenesis and senescence — none of these are methylation-dependent.

For unmethylated patients, the honest picture is that TMZ is less effective as a standalone treatment — which makes every other element of this protocol more important, not less. The metabolic pressure, the cannabinoid apoptosis, the immune support, and the strategic reserve of mebendazole as a radiosensitiser all carry greater relative weight when chemotherapy alone is less reliable. Unmethylated status changes the emphasis within the protocol — it does not diminish its relevance.

There are also emerging treatments — immunotherapy, personalised neoantigen vaccines, and novel TMZ-sensitising combinations — that are showing particular promise specifically in unmethylated patients. The research landscape is moving. Do not read a biomarker result as a verdict. Read it as information that shapes your strategy.

The most important thing to understand is that GBM has multiple vulnerabilities — metabolic, inflammatory, angiogenic, immunological. Standard treatment targets some of them. An informed patient can target all of them simultaneously.

Section Three

The Mindset — Why This Comes First

This section comes before the science deliberately. Because without the right mindset, none of the rest of it is sustainable.

No Regrets Over Perfect Outcomes

There are no guarantees with GBM. The goal is not to guarantee survival — it is to ensure that whatever happens, you have left no stone unturned. If you do everything available to you and it is not enough, you will have no regrets. That clarity is itself a form of peace — and it is the foundation everything else is built on.

The Positive Outlook Is Not Soft Science

Chronic psychological stress elevates cortisol and activates sympathetic signalling, which together suppress immune surveillance of tumour cells. A positive outlook directly reduces cortisol and strengthens the immune function that GBM works hardest to evade. Joy, holidays, connection, laughter — these are not separate from the treatment. They are part of it.

Mindfulness — Even If You're Not Naturally Positive

Not everyone facing a GBM diagnosis is naturally optimistic — and that is entirely understandable. Mindfulness offers a practical, evidence-based alternative that does not require positivity as a starting point. The practice of noticing small things — genuinely appreciating their existence — is both grounding and surprisingly powerful. A tree. A cup of tea. A moment of sunlight. These are not trivial observations. They are active interruptions to the spiral of catastrophic thinking that a terminal diagnosis can trigger. Mindfulness also provides a reliable distraction method when anxiety begins to build — redirecting attention outward rather than inward.

Guided Meditation — Getting Through the Early Months

The months immediately following diagnosis — and recurrence — involve a level of shock, anxiety and sleeplessness that is genuinely unlike anything most people have experienced. Guided meditation apps, particularly Calm, proved invaluable in the early months. They helped process the initial shock, manage sustained anxiety, and — practically — get back to sleep after night terrors, which are a real and underreported experience for many GBM patients. Calm and similar apps (Headspace, Insight Timer) are inexpensive, immediately accessible, and require no prior meditation experience. A sleep meditation at bedtime became a consistent and effective part of Pete's routine. Three years on, it remains an occasional tool rather than a daily necessity — which is itself a measure of how far things have come.

Brain Training and Cognitive Protection

Radiotherapy and chemotherapy carry real cognitive consequences — and it is important to know that radiation necrosis (the brain's inflammatory response to radiotherapy) does not stop at the end of treatment. It continues for months or years afterward, meaning cognitive effects may emerge or worsen well after the treatment that caused them has finished. Rather than treating this as an inevitable loss, approach it as a problem requiring active solutions.

Daily brain training — apps like Lumosity, Elevate or even simple puzzles and memory games — provides measurable cognitive stimulation that appears to support neural resilience. Writing lists for tasks, appointments and decisions is not a sign of decline — it is a sensible adaptation that frees cognitive load for more important things. This habit, started early, becomes natural and protective. The principle is simple: rather than lamenting what the treatment may affect, find practical systems that compensate for it and preserve function.

Adherence Beats Perfection

A sustainable protocol you maintain for years is vastly superior to a perfect protocol you abandon after weeks. Every element of the approach described here was chosen partly because it can be maintained long-term by a real person living a real life. Clinical data on GBM dietary adherence shows that patients maintaining protocols for 6+ months had dramatically better survival than those who did not. Duration matters more than perfection.

Build the Relationship With Your Oncologist

The single most protective factor in this entire approach — beyond any drug or supplement — is having an oncologist who takes your research seriously, engages with it honestly, and monitors you appropriately. Present your research clearly, show your evidence, and build that collaboration. It changes everything.

Hydration — The Overlooked Essential

Adequate hydration is consistently underappreciated in cancer care. Dehydration impairs cognitive function, reduces physical performance, affects drug metabolism and absorption, and compromises the kidney function that chemotherapy depends on being maintained. For GBM patients on a complex multi-drug protocol, maintaining consistent fluid intake throughout the day is a simple and cost-free intervention with measurable physiological benefit. A practical target is 2–2.5 litres of water daily, adjusted upward on exercise days. Starting the morning with a large glass of water before anything else is a habit worth establishing from day one.

Start Every Morning With Protein

A protein-first breakfast — before carbohydrates, before coffee, before anything else — establishes blood glucose stability from the first meal of the day, supports lean mass preservation, and provides the amino acid substrate for neurotransmitter production that influences mood and cognitive function throughout the morning. Eggs, Greek yoghurt, smoked salmon, protein shakes — the specific source matters less than the habit of prioritising protein as the first nutritional input of the day. This is a simple, sustainable and effective anchor for the entire metabolic strategy.

Recognising Anxiety — And Not Letting It Spiral

Even three years on, there are moments. A period of poor sleep, an uncomfortable environment, a disrupted routine — these can trigger physical symptoms that feel alarming: muscle spasms, tension, racing thoughts. The critical skill is pattern recognition — learning to distinguish anxiety manifesting physically from something neurological, and responding appropriately rather than catastrophising.

Patients should actively learn their own body's behaviour patterns within treatment cycles. Certain side effects, sensations and mood changes are predictable and cyclical — understanding this calendar of your own physiology removes much of the fear. Paranoia about symptoms is a real and underreported challenge in GBM patients, particularly during and after radiotherapy. The antidote is knowledge, pattern recognition, and a pre-agreed response plan with your clinical team.

When anxiety peaks — three steps: Stop. Take three slow, deliberate deep breaths with an extended exhale. Name what is happening: "This is anxiety. It will pass." That simple act of labelling interrupts the feedback loop before it escalates.

You are not a statistic. The median survival figure describes an average across thousands of patients. They are flagged here as options worth researching and discussing with your oncologist, with the honest caveat that the evidence base is early-stage and the cost-benefit calculation is personal. If financial resources allow for only one significant expenditure beyond the affordable supplement stack, cannabinoids have the stronger evidence base for GBM specifically.
Section Four

The Metabolic Strategy — Placing Pressure on the Tumour

GBM cells are heavily dependent on glucose through a mechanism called the Warburg Effect — they burn sugar even when oxygen is available, unlike normal cells. This creates a direct therapeutic target: consistently lowering blood glucose places sustained metabolic pressure on the tumour, exploiting a vulnerability that normal cells do not share.

The key measurement is the Glucose-Ketone Index (GKI) — the ratio of blood glucose to ketone bodies. A lower GKI means less glucose and more ketones in circulation. Normal cells can run efficiently on ketones. GBM cells largely cannot.

🥩

Very Low Carb Diet

35–45g carbohydrates daily as the baseline target. This is not full ketogenic — it is sustainable long-term, which matters more. A diet you maintain for years beats a stricter diet abandoned after weeks.

📅

Flexibility Ceiling

A 60g ceiling for up to 12 weeks per year allows for holidays, social occasions and eating out. Adherence over time is the goal — not perfection on every single day.

⏰

Intermittent Fasting

12–16 hour fasting windows, at least twice weekly. Deepens ketosis, activates AMPK, enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Achievable by extending the overnight fast.

🥥

MCT C8 Oil

One tablespoon daily with breakfast. Caprylic acid (C8) is the most ketogenic MCT, rapidly converted to beta-hydroxybutyrate — raising ketones even on days where carb intake is slightly higher.

Important: The carbohydrate number is a proxy for the GKI — the actual target. Food quality matters enormously. 60g of carbohydrates from vegetables, berries and legumes produces a very different metabolic response than 60g from processed foods. Always prioritise food quality alongside quantity.

Protein intake should be prioritised at every meal — a minimum of 1.6–2g per kg of bodyweight daily. GBM treatment creates real risk of muscle loss (sarcopenia), which independently worsens outcomes. Protecting lean mass is a clinical priority, not just an aesthetic one.

Why Very Low Carb Rather Than Strict Ketogenic

Strict ketogenic diets — typically defined as below 20g of carbohydrates daily at a 4:1 fat-to-protein ratio — are clinically effective at lowering the GKI but practically brutal to sustain. The food options are severely restricted, social eating becomes genuinely difficult, and the psychological burden of rigid dietary rules on top of a cancer diagnosis can itself become a source of chronic stress — which, as we have established, is counterproductive for immune function. Many patients start strict keto with good intentions and abandon it within weeks, which produces worse outcomes than a sustainable moderate approach maintained for years.

A very low carb approach — 35–45g daily — achieves the majority of the metabolic benefit with considerably more food variety, social flexibility, and long-term viability. The exercise, intermittent fasting, metformin and MCT oil all enhance the GKI further, meaning the dietary component does not need to carry the entire metabolic burden alone. This is a team effort across multiple interventions — not a single dietary constraint doing all the work.

Advanced Consideration
Prolonged Fasting — Could Longer Periods Be Advantageous?
Early Clinical

The 12–16 hour intermittent fasting protocol described in this document is the recommended sustainable baseline — achievable twice weekly without meaningful physiological cost. However the evidence suggests that longer fasting periods, when appropriately timed and clinically supervised, may offer additional metabolic benefit worth understanding.

✅ Why It Could Be Advantageous

A prospective GBM case series found that patients completing repeated prolonged fasts averaging 6 days achieved a mean GKI of 1.28 — compared to 5.10 on ketogenic diet alone and 3.22 overall. This is a level of metabolic pressure on the tumour that diet alone simply cannot achieve.

The Differential Stress Resistance (DSR) mechanism is significantly amplified during prolonged fasting — normal cells enter deep protective mode while GBM cells, locked into glucose dependency, become maximally vulnerable. When timed around chemotherapy or radiotherapy, this widens the therapeutic window between tumour damage and healthy tissue protection to its greatest possible extent.

Immune mobilisation — particularly NK cell and cytotoxic T cell activity — is also enhanced during prolonged fasting beyond what shorter windows achieve, potentially strengthening the immune surveillance that GBM works hardest to suppress.

⚠️ Why Timing Needs Careful Consideration

Sarcopenia risk: Beyond 24 hours, the body increasingly breaks down muscle protein for energy. For patients already managing muscle loss from chemotherapy, this is a meaningful clinical concern — particularly for those working hard to maintain lean mass.

Weight management: The GBM case series noted weight loss of 11.2% of baseline bodyweight across the protocol. Patients who are already at or below their target weight should be particularly cautious. Maintaining lean mass during treatment is a clinical priority that prolonged fasting can undermine.

Chemotherapy cycle timing: A 3-day fast undertaken randomly — disconnected from treatment timing — misses the primary therapeutic opportunity. The greatest benefit comes from fasting in the window surrounding TMZ administration, where DSR amplifies chemotherapy's differential effect on tumour versus healthy cells.

Quality of life: Fatigue, irritability and light-headedness are consistently reported adverse effects of prolonged fasting in GBM patients. These are manageable but require planning, support and appropriate clinical oversight.

💬 The Oncologist Conversation Worth Having

If prolonged fasting appeals — and the GKI data makes it genuinely compelling — the appropriate framing is a single strategically timed 3-day fast coordinated around a TMZ cycle, rather than a regular fasting protocol. The specific questions worth raising with your oncologist are:

→ Which point in my TMZ cycle would maximise the DSR benefit of a 3-day fast?

→ Given my current weight and lean mass, is this appropriate at this stage of treatment?

→ Should electrolyte supplementation be maintained during the fast to protect kidney function on TMZ?

→ Would essential amino acid supplementation during the fast preserve lean mass without breaking the metabolic benefit?

The 12–16 hour protocol described in this document remains the recommended sustainable baseline. Prolonged fasting is an advanced tool with genuine evidence — not a starting point, but a strategic option worth understanding and discussing when the clinical context is right.

Section Five

Exercise — More Powerful Than It Appears

Exercise is not a peripheral lifestyle recommendation in this framework. It is a direct metabolic and immunological intervention with specific mechanisms relevant to GBM.

🚶

Daily Walking

10,000–15,000 steps daily at moderate pace. Continuously depletes glycogen, improving insulin sensitivity and lowering blood glucose — directly improving GKI throughout the day.

💪

Resistance Training

Twice weekly. The single most important intervention for preserving lean mass against the catabolic pressure of chemotherapy, cannabinoids, metformin and very low carb diet simultaneously.

🔬

Immune Mobilisation

Exercise mobilises cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells — the immune cells responsible for identifying and destroying tumour cells. This is not metaphorical. It is measurable.

🧠

Molecular Pathways

Exercise influences key molecular pathways involved in GBM progression, including angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming — the same pathways targeted pharmacologically by irbesartan and metformin.

The combination of fasted walking on IF days is particularly powerful — low-intensity fasted aerobic activity rapidly depletes glycogen, elevates ketones, and produces the most favourable GKI of any combination in the protocol. For patients managing seizure risk, low-intensity fasted walking is also considerably safer than high-intensity fasted training.

⚖️ A Note on the ARISTOCRAT Trial — and an Honest Question About Participation

The ARISTOCRAT trial is double-blind and placebo-controlled — meaning one in three participants receives a placebo rather than active cannabinoids. This is standard scientific methodology, designed to eliminate bias and produce clean causal data. The reasoning is entirely valid in most medical contexts.

What the Phase I data actually showed: The earlier Phase I trial in 27 patients produced a striking result — after two years, 50% of patients in the nabiximols arm were still alive, compared to only 22% of those on TMZ plus placebo. Against a backdrop where average survival after recurrence is less than 10 months, this signal is remarkable. The ARISTOCRAT Phase II trial is designed to confirm whether this benefit holds at scale. These are the numbers that make the 1-in-3 placebo allocation so difficult to accept for an informed patient who has already reviewed the evidence.

But GBM is not most medical contexts. And this is a question every patient offered ARISTOCRAT participation deserves to think about carefully and honestly.

What placebo participation actually means: A 1-in-3 chance of receiving a placebo when your median survival is 14 months is not an abstract statistical inconvenience. It potentially means months of your life — the window in which cannabinoids at therapeutic doses might be making a meaningful difference — spent on an inactive substance. For a patient who has researched the mechanism and concluded the evidence justifies the intervention, that is an extraordinarily high personal cost to pay for scientific rigour that benefits future patients rather than yourself.

The blinding problem: There is a more specific issue that is rarely acknowledged openly. Double-blinding in high-dose THC trials almost certainly does not work in practice. A patient receiving therapeutic doses of THC via buccal delivery knows they are not on placebo — the psychoactive effects are unmistakeable. The trial therefore loses blinding integrity in the active arm anyway, while patients in the placebo arm lose their opportunity to access a potentially meaningful intervention. This represents a significant methodological limitation alongside the ethical one.

The equipoise question: Clinical trials are ethically justified only when genuine uncertainty exists about which arm is superior — a principle called equipoise. Given the ARISTOCRAT Phase I data showing a hazard ratio below 0.5 for cannabinoids alongside TMZ, that equipoise is increasingly difficult to defend for MGMT-methylated patients specifically.

This is not an argument against science. Rigorous trials are how medicine advances and how future patients benefit. But informed consent means understanding exactly what trial participation involves — including a candid assessment of what the placebo arm means for you personally, given your specific prognosis and timeline. Every patient must make this decision for themselves, in full possession of the facts. The question worth asking your oncologist is simple: given what the Phase I data already shows, would you accept a 1-in-3 chance of placebo?

🔬 Why This Ratio — The Mechanistic Reasoning

The ratio of THC:CBD:CBG at approximately 4.7:1.4:1 (140mg:42mg:30mg per component daily) is not arbitrary. Each cannabinoid targets a fundamentally different set of receptors and cellular mechanisms — meaning the ratio reflects the relative weighting of those mechanisms against GBM biology, balanced against practical tolerability.

Why THC is dominant (140mg daily): THC is the primary apoptosis driver in this protocol. It binds with high affinity to CB1 receptors — which are heavily overexpressed in GBM tumour cells and GBM stem cells — triggering a cascade that leads directly to programmed cell death. At apoptosis-level concentrations (above approximately 100mg), THC also acts independently of CB1/CB2 receptors through direct mitochondrial membrane disruption, creating a secondary apoptotic pathway. No other cannabinoid achieves this dual mechanism at equivalent concentrations. THC is therefore the highest-weighted component because it carries the greatest direct anti-tumour burden — provided patient tolerability allows it to be reached and sustained.

Why CBD is the secondary component (42mg daily): CBD targets GPR55 and TRPV1 receptors — pathways entirely distinct from THC's CB1/CB2 route — and triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress leading to a separate apoptotic cascade. Critically, CBD has a specific and well-evidenced role in overcoming TMZ resistance: it inhibits MGMT overexpression and disrupts DNA mismatch repair pathways that GBM uses to survive chemotherapy. For MGMT-methylated patients, this TMZ-sensitising property makes CBD particularly valuable. CBD also modulates THC's psychoactive effects at the CB1 receptor — meaning adequate CBD in the ratio partially offsets the tolerability challenge of high-dose THC, allowing patients to reach higher THC doses than they might otherwise tolerate. This dual role — anti-tumour and tolerability-enabling — justifies CBD as the second-weighted component.

Why CBG completes the picture (30mg daily): CBG operates primarily through GPR55 — shared with CBD but through different downstream signalling — and uniquely arrests the cell cycle at both G1/S1 and G2/M checkpoints. This cytostatic mechanism (stopping cells from dividing) is complementary to, rather than duplicating, the apoptotic mechanisms of THC and CBD. CBG is particularly active against GBM stem cells — the highly treatment-resistant subpopulation responsible for recurrence after surgery and chemotherapy. Targeting stem cells directly addresses one of GBM's most clinically significant survival mechanisms. CBG is the lowest-weighted component because its mechanism is cytostatic rather than directly lethal to tumour cells — but its stem cell activity makes it irreplaceable in the combination.

The combined effect: Together, the three cannabinoids cover CB1, CB2, GPR55 and TRPV1 receptors simultaneously, trigger apoptosis through at least three independent pathways, arrest the cell cycle, target GBM stem cells, sensitise the tumour to TMZ, and partially self-regulate tolerability. No single cannabinoid achieves more than a fraction of this — the ratio is the therapy.

📈 How to Get There — The Titration Method

The doses described above — 140mg THC, 42mg CBD, 30mg CBG daily — are maintenance therapeutic doses reached after a period of gradual dose escalation. No patient should begin at these levels. Starting at therapeutic dose would be both unsafe and counterproductive — THC in particular requires the body and brain to adapt progressively.

The titration approach used in this protocol was inspired by the ARISTOCRAT clinical trial's self-titration principle — where patients increase dose incrementally based on personal tolerability — but adapted to maintain proportional ratios across all three cannabinoids throughout the escalation process. The ARISTOCRAT trial used a fixed 1:1 THC:CBD ratio (Sativex/nabiximols) and allowed patients to titrate to their own ceiling. This protocol starts from a 1:1:1 ratio across all three cannabinoids and scales all three proportionally — preserving the mechanistic balance at every stage of escalation.

The Titration Protocol — Step by Step
Week 1–2
Begin at equal low doses — approximately 5mg THC : 5mg CBD : 5mg CBG daily, taken via buccal delivery, split morning and evening. This is the 1:1:1 starting point. The goal is zero psychoactive effect at this stage — simply establishing the routine and confirming basic tolerability.
Week 3–4
If week 1–2 was well tolerated with no adverse effects, increase all three by approximately 25–30% proportionally. The ratio shifts here — begin weighting THC upward relative to CBD and CBG as you scale, moving toward the target ratio. Mild psychoactive effect from THC may begin to appear — this is expected and guides the pace of further escalation.
Weeks 5–12
Continue increasing all three cannabinoids by 20–25% every two weeks, progressively shifting toward the target THC:CBD:CBG ratio as THC is scaled faster than CBD and CBG. Tolerability at each stage guides whether to proceed, pause or reduce slightly before continuing. Evening doses can be increased faster than morning doses — most patients tolerate higher THC at night when psychoactive effects are less disruptive.
Week 12+
Approach and consolidate at the target maintenance dose — 140mg THC : 42mg CBD : 30mg CBG daily — only if tolerability at preceding levels has been fully established. Some patients will reach a personal ceiling below this target. That ceiling is their therapeutic dose. A sustained lower dose is significantly more valuable than an unsustainable higher one.

The most important principle of titration: tolerability is the rate-limiting factor, not a target timeline. Some patients will reach maintenance dose in 8 weeks. Others may take 6 months. The pace is entirely individual and entirely guided by the patient's own response. There is no clinical benefit to rushing — and significant risk in doing so.

What to expect during escalation: Mild psychoactive effects from THC — altered perception, drowsiness, mild anxiety — are normal during dose increases and typically diminish as tolerance develops. If effects are significant or distressing, reduce to the previous dose and remain there for an additional two weeks before attempting further escalation. CBD's presence in the ratio helps moderate these effects — one of the practical reasons CBD is maintained alongside THC throughout.

Buccal delivery throughout: All doses should be administered via the buccal (oromucosal) route — held against the cheek or under the tongue rather than swallowed. This bypasses first-pass liver metabolism, producing more consistent and predictable blood levels than oral capsules and making the titration process more reliable. Swallowing cannabinoids produces highly variable absorption that makes meaningful titration almost impossible.

Section Eight

Surveillance & Monitoring

The recurrence in August 2025 was caught early precisely because of consistent 3-monthly MRI surveillance. Early detection of recurrence — when tumour burden is smallest and surgical options are greatest — is itself a survival intervention.

Push for 3-Monthly Scans

Some oncologists default to 6-monthly surveillance post-treatment. The evidence for early recurrence detection improving outcomes is clear. Advocate for 3-monthly MRI as standard. If you have already had a recurrence, consider discussing 6–8 weekly scans in the high-risk window immediately following treatment completion.

Daily Self-Monitoring

Daily tracking of body composition (smart scales), activity (smart watch/ring), and nutritional intake (calories, protein, carbohydrates) provides early warning of anything drifting in the wrong direction — weeks before it would show up clinically. Lean mass loss in particular is a signal requiring immediate dietary adjustment.

Blood Monitoring

Regular liver function tests (LFTs) are essential given the combination of simvastatin, high-dose supplements and cannabinoids. Metformin requires renal monitoring. Your oncologist will manage this — but understanding why these tests matter helps you engage with them actively.

Glossary of Terms

Plain English definitions for every technical term used in this document, with a note on why each matters for GBM patients. Terms are listed alphabetically.

Adjuvant Therapy
Treatment given after the main treatment — in GBM, this typically means chemotherapy (TMZ) continued for months after surgery and radiotherapy have been completed. The goal is to mop up any remaining microscopic tumour cells.
→ The 12-month adjuvant TMZ protocol described in this document is standard post-operative care for GBM. ↑ back
AMPK
AMP-Activated Protein Kinase
A master energy-sensing switch inside cells. When AMPK is activated, cells shift into energy-conservation mode — suppressing growth and cell division. Fasting, exercise and metformin all activate AMPK.
→ Activating AMPK suppresses the mTOR pathway that GBM uses to drive tumour growth. Multiple elements of this protocol target AMPK simultaneously. ↑ back
Angiogenesis
The process by which tumours grow new blood vessels to feed themselves. Without a blood supply, tumours cannot grow beyond a small size. GBM is particularly aggressive at recruiting new blood vessels.
→ Irbesartan and other drugs in this protocol specifically target angiogenesis — cutting off the tumour's blood supply. ↑ back
Apoptosis
Programmed cell death — essentially a cell's built-in self-destruct mechanism. Healthy cells use apoptosis to remove damaged or abnormal cells. Cancer cells find ways to switch this mechanism off, allowing them to survive when they should die.
→ Triggering apoptosis in GBM cells is one of the primary goals of the cannabinoid protocol. THC, CBD and CBG each activate apoptosis through different pathways. ↑ back
ARB
Angiotensin Receptor Blocker
A class of drugs that block angiotensin II receptors — primarily used to treat high blood pressure. In GBM, these receptors are overexpressed on tumour cells, making ARBs a potential anti-tumour agent beyond their blood pressure function.
→ Irbesartan is an ARB. Patients without high blood pressure should ask their oncologist about its anti-angiogenic properties in GBM. ↑ back
BBB
Blood-Brain Barrier
A highly selective protective barrier between the bloodstream and the brain. It prevents most substances — including many drugs — from reaching brain tissue. This is one of the biggest challenges in treating brain tumours: many potentially effective drugs simply cannot get through.
→ BBB penetration is a critical selection criterion for drugs in this protocol. Simvastatin was chosen partly because it crosses the BBB more effectively than other statins. ↑ back
Benzimidazoles
A class of drugs originally developed as antiparasitic agents — essentially worming tablets used in humans (mebendazole) and animals (fenbendazole). Research has found that they also disrupt tumour cell biology through several mechanisms including blocking cell division and cutting off blood supply.
→ Mebendazole specifically has Phase I/II clinical trial evidence in GBM, particularly as a radiosensitiser alongside radiotherapy. ↑ back
Biomarker
A measurable biological indicator — a gene, protein or other molecule — that provides information about a disease or how a patient might respond to treatment. In GBM, MGMT methylation status is the most important biomarker.
→ Ask your oncologist about your tumour's biomarker profile as early as possible — it shapes the entire treatment strategy. ↑ back
CBG
Cannabigerol
One of over 100 cannabinoids found in the cannabis plant. Unlike THC, CBG is non-psychoactive. It acts on different receptors to THC and CBD — particularly GPR55 — and has been shown to arrest GBM cell division at specific points in the cell cycle.
→ CBG complements THC and CBD by targeting GBM stem cells — the particularly resistant cells responsible for tumour regrowth after treatment. ↑ back
CBD
Cannabidiol
A non-psychoactive cannabinoid found in cannabis. Acts on different receptors to THC, including TRPV1 and GPR55. Has been shown to overcome two of GBM's primary resistance mechanisms to chemotherapy — MGMT overexpression and DNA mismatch repair deficiency.
→ CBD's ability to enhance TMZ sensitivity is particularly valuable in MGMT-methylated patients and is the focus of the ARISTOCRAT clinical trial. ↑ back

Human medical research is conducted in structured phases. Phase I tests safety and dosage in a small group. Phase II tests whether a treatment shows signs of working. Phase III is a large randomised controlled trial comparing the new treatment to existing standard care. Phase IV monitors long-term effects after approval.
→ Understanding trial phases helps patients evaluate how strong the evidence is for any given intervention in this protocol. ↑ back
COX-2
Cyclooxygenase-2
An enzyme that drives inflammation and is overexpressed in many cancers including GBM. Blocking COX-2 reduces the inflammatory environment that tumours exploit to grow and evade the immune system.
→ Curcumin, boswellia, omega-3 and melatonin all suppress COX-2 through different mechanisms — creating overlapping anti-inflammatory pressure. ↑ back
Cortisol
The body's primary stress hormone. In short bursts it is essential and useful. Chronically elevated cortisol — from sustained psychological stress — suppresses immune function, increases inflammation, and has been linked to worse outcomes in cancer patients.
→ This is why the positive mindset component of this protocol is not separate from the medical treatment — it directly affects cortisol levels and immune surveillance. ↑ back

Coordinated Undermining of Survival Paths
A clinical trial protocol that combines nine repurposed drugs alongside TMZ to attack GBM through multiple pathways simultaneously. It established that multi-drug adjuvant protocols are safe and tolerable in GBM patients — validating the principle behind the approach described in this document.
→ CUSP9v3 provided clinical proof-of-concept that combining multiple repurposed drugs alongside standard chemotherapy is both feasible and well-tolerated. ↑ back
Differential Stress Resistance (DSR)
The phenomenon whereby fasting protects normal cells from the toxic effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, while leaving cancer cells more vulnerable. Normal cells can adapt to low-nutrient conditions; cancer cells, locked into their abnormal metabolic programmes, cannot.
→ This is the scientific basis for timing intermittent fasting around chemotherapy administration — it widens the gap between what treatment does to tumour cells versus healthy cells. ↑ back
ER Stress
Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress
The endoplasmic reticulum is a structure inside cells responsible for protein production and folding. When it is overwhelmed or disrupted, it triggers a stress response that can lead to cell death (apoptosis). Several compounds in this protocol deliberately induce ER stress in tumour cells.
→ Sulforaphane and cannabinoids both induce ER stress in GBM cells as part of their apoptotic mechanism. ↑ back
GBM / Glioblastoma
Glioblastoma Multiforme — Grade IV
The most aggressive form of primary brain cancer, arising from glial cells — the supporting cells of the brain. Grade IV is the highest grade, indicating rapid growth and aggressive behaviour. It is characterised by resistance to treatment, tendency to recur, and the ability to invade surrounding brain tissue.
→ Understanding GBM's specific biology — its metabolic dependencies, immune evasion strategies and resistance mechanisms — is the foundation of everything in this document. ↑ back
GKI
Glucose-Ketone Index
A simple ratio: blood glucose (in mmol/L) divided by blood ketones (in mmol/L). A lower GKI means less glucose and more ketones in the bloodstream — a less favourable environment for GBM, which depends heavily on glucose. A GKI below 2 is considered the therapeutic target zone.
→ The GKI is the single most useful number for monitoring how well the metabolic protocol is working. Home blood glucose and ketone monitors make this measurable daily. ↑ back
Glutamine
An amino acid that serves as a secondary fuel source for GBM alongside glucose. When glucose is restricted, some tumours can adapt to use glutamine more heavily — which is why glucose restriction alone, while important, works best as part of a broader metabolic strategy.
→ Lamotrigine's glutamate-reducing properties are relevant here — glutamate and glutamine share metabolic pathways that GBM exploits for invasion. ↑ back
GPR55
G Protein-Coupled Receptor 55
A receptor found on cell surfaces that is overexpressed in GBM — particularly on GBM stem cells. CBD and CBG bind to this receptor as part of their anti-tumour mechanism, making it a specific target for cannabinoid therapy in brain tumours.
→ GPR55 is one of the reasons CBD and CBG are specifically valuable in GBM rather than just generally anti-cancer — this receptor is particularly relevant to brain tumour biology. ↑ back
IDH
Isocitrate Dehydrogenase
A gene that is frequently mutated in lower-grade gliomas but is typically wildtype (unmutated) in GBM. IDH status is a key diagnostic and prognostic marker — IDH-wildtype GBM is the most aggressive form and the type described in this document.
→ Ask your oncologist about your IDH status alongside MGMT — together they define your tumour's molecular profile and influence treatment decisions. ↑ back
IF
Intermittent Fasting
A pattern of eating that cycles between periods of fasting and eating. In this protocol, 12–16 hour fasting windows are used at least twice weekly — typically achieved by extending the overnight fast and delaying breakfast. No food is consumed during the fasting window, though water, black coffee and fat-only additions (like MCT oil for drug absorption) are considered compatible.
→ IF lowers blood glucose, raises ketones, activates AMPK, and — crucially — enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy and radiotherapy through the differential stress resistance mechanism. ↑ back
In Vitro / In Vivo
In vitro means research conducted in a laboratory using cells in a dish or test tube — outside a living organism. In vivo means research conducted in a living organism, typically an animal model. Both are forms of preclinical research — important for establishing mechanisms, but not the same as evidence from human clinical trials.
→ Much of the supplement and cannabinoid evidence in this document is in vitro or in vivo. This is promising but should be understood as early-stage evidence pending larger human trials. ↑ back
LFTs
Liver Function Tests
A standard blood test that measures enzymes and proteins produced by the liver, indicating how well it is functioning. Several drugs and supplements in this protocol — particularly simvastatin and high-dose mebendazole — can stress the liver and require LFT monitoring to detect any problems early.
→ Regular LFT monitoring is essential for anyone following this protocol. Your oncologist should be managing this as part of routine blood testing. ↑ back
MCT Oil
Medium-Chain Triglyceride Oil
A type of fat derived from coconut oil that is rapidly converted by the liver into ketone bodies — an alternative fuel source to glucose. C8 (caprylic acid) is the most ketogenic form, meaning it produces the most ketones per gram consumed.
→ MCT C8 oil raises ketone levels even on days where carbohydrate intake is slightly higher than usual, helping maintain a favourable GKI. It also aids absorption of fat-soluble supplements and mebendazole. ↑ back
Mevalonate Pathway
A metabolic pathway inside cells responsible for producing cholesterol and other molecules needed for cell growth. Cancer cells rely heavily on this pathway. Statins block it — which is why, beyond their cholesterol-lowering effect, they have potential anti-tumour properties.
→ Critically, GBM treatment (radiation and TMZ) actually activates the mevalonate pathway in surviving tumour cells — creating a specific vulnerability that simvastatin is well-positioned to exploit. ↑ back
MGMT Methylation
O6-Methylguanine-DNA Methyltransferase
MGMT is a DNA repair protein. When the MGMT gene is methylated (chemically silenced), the tumour cannot repair the DNA damage caused by TMZ chemotherapy — meaning the chemotherapy works much more effectively. Approximately 40% of GBM patients have this methylation. The other 60% have MGMT-unmethylated tumours that are more resistant to TMZ.
→ MGMT methylation status is the single most important biomarker in GBM. Several interventions in this protocol — CBD, metformin, cannabinoids — are particularly effective in MGMT-methylated patients. ↑ back
mTOR
Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin
A master regulator of cell growth, proliferation and metabolism inside cells. When mTOR is active, cells grow and divide. Cancer cells frequently hijack mTOR signalling to drive uncontrolled growth. Suppressing mTOR is a key anti-tumour strategy.
→ Metformin, sulforaphane, curcumin and intermittent fasting all suppress mTOR through different mechanisms — this convergence on a single critical pathway is one of the protocol's key strengths. ↑ back
NF-κB
Nuclear Factor Kappa B
A protein complex that acts as a master switch for inflammation inside cells. In GBM, NF-κB is chronically overactivated — driving a pro-tumour inflammatory environment that promotes growth, invasion and resistance to treatment.
→ Curcumin, boswellia, quercetin, omega-3 and melatonin all suppress NF-κB — one of the most important inflammatory targets in GBM biology. ↑ back
NK Cells
Natural Killer Cells
A type of immune cell that identifies and destroys abnormal cells including tumour cells, without needing prior exposure to learn what to attack. They are part of the innate immune system — the body's first line of defence. GBM actively suppresses NK cell function to evade immune destruction.
→ Exercise specifically mobilises NK cells into circulation — one of the most direct mechanisms by which physical activity contributes to anti-tumour immune surveillance. ↑ back
NLRP3
NOD-like Receptor Protein 3
A component of the inflammasome — a molecular machine inside immune cells that triggers inflammation. Overactivation of NLRP3 creates a chronic inflammatory environment that tumours exploit. It is one of the three major inflammatory pathways (alongside NF-κB and COX-2) targeted by this protocol's anti-inflammatory supplements.
→ Quercetin and omega-3 specifically target NLRP3 alongside their other anti-inflammatory mechanisms. ↑ back
Parietal Lobe
One of the four main lobes of the brain, located towards the top and back of the head. The left parietal lobe processes sensory information from the right side of the body — including touch, pressure and spatial awareness. A tumour in this area can affect right-sided function, including leg movement and sensation.
→ The location of this patient's tumour in the left parietal lobe explains the right leg deficit that followed surgery — the brain's left side controls the right side of the body. ↑ back
Preclinical Evidence
Research conducted in laboratories using cell cultures (in vitro) or animal models (in vivo) — before testing in humans. Preclinical evidence is essential for establishing mechanisms and safety signals, but cannot be assumed to translate directly to human outcomes. It represents the first rung of the evidence ladder.
→ Several supplements and cannabinoid findings in this document are supported by preclinical evidence. This is clearly labelled throughout — promising findings that warrant clinical investigation rather than proven human therapies. ↑ back
PSK / PSP
Polysaccharide-K / Polysaccharide-Peptide
Bioactive compounds extracted from Turkey Tail mushroom (Trametes versicolor). PSK in particular has randomised controlled trial evidence in oncology — primarily in gastric and colorectal cancer — showing improved survival alongside chemotherapy through immune modulation.
→ Turkey Tail's PSK/PSP content makes it the best-evidenced immune-modulating mushroom for oncology use, with dose escalation during active chemotherapy being particularly well-supported. ↑ back
Radiosensitiser
A substance that makes tumour cells more sensitive to the damaging effects of radiation therapy — meaning radiotherapy becomes more effective at the same dose. Radiosensitisers ideally affect tumour cells more than healthy tissue.
→ Mebendazole has shown radiosensitising properties in GBM preclinical models — one of the key reasons it is being held in reserve for a potential future radiotherapy scenario. ↑ back
Repurposed Drug
An existing drug — approved and in clinical use for one condition — that research suggests may also have beneficial effects in a different condition. Repurposed drugs have major advantages: their safety profiles are already established in humans, they are often inexpensive, and they can be prescribed off-label by willing clinicians.
→ Metformin, simvastatin, irbesartan, lamotrigine and mebendazole are all repurposed drugs in the context of this protocol — none were developed for GBM, but all have evidence supporting their use in it. ↑ back
Resection
Surgical removal of a tumour. Maximal safe resection means removing as much tumour as possible without causing unacceptable damage to surrounding brain function. It is important to understand that no GBM resection achieves truly complete removal — microscopic tumour cells always remain beyond the limits of surgical visibility and imaging resolution.
→ Maximal safe resection is the strongest prognostic surgical factor in GBM — the less visible tumour that remains, the better the starting point for all subsequent treatment. ↑ back
Sarcopenia
The loss of muscle mass and strength. In cancer patients, sarcopenia is a serious concern — it independently worsens outcomes, reduces treatment tolerance, and slows recovery. GBM treatment (chemotherapy, very low calorie diets, cannabinoids at high doses) all create sarcopenia risk.
→ Resistance training twice weekly and high protein intake (1.6–2g per kg of bodyweight daily) are the two most important interventions for preventing sarcopenia in this protocol. ↑ back
Senescence
A state in which cells permanently stop dividing but do not die. In cancer, inducing senescence in tumour cells prevents them from multiplying — effectively freezing the tumour in place. This is a different mechanism from apoptosis (which kills cells) but equally valuable as a therapeutic strategy.
→ The combination of metformin and simvastatin drives GBM cells into senescence — a mechanism entirely distinct from the apoptosis triggered by cannabinoids, giving the protocol two complementary ways to stop tumour growth. ↑ back
SOC / STUPP Protocol
Standard of Care
The standard treatment approach for newly diagnosed GBM — named after Roger Stupp whose trial established it. It consists of maximal safe surgical resection, followed by six weeks of concurrent radiotherapy and daily temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy, followed by six monthly cycles of adjuvant TMZ. This remains the backbone of GBM treatment worldwide.
→ Everything described in this document is designed to work alongside SOC — enhancing it, not replacing it. Standard of care remains the essential foundation. ↑ back
THC
Tetrahydrocannabinol
The primary psychoactive compound in cannabis. Binds strongly to CB1 receptors (predominantly in the brain) and CB2 receptors (predominantly in immune tissue) — both overexpressed in GBM. At sufficient concentrations, THC induces apoptosis in GBM cells. Its psychoactive effects mean dose escalation must be gradual and patient tolerability is the rate-limiting factor.
→ THC's legal status varies by country. In the UK, medicinal THC requires a specialist prescription. Evening-weighted dosing minimises daytime psychoactive effects while maintaining therapeutic exposure. ↑ back
TMZ
Temozolomide
The chemotherapy drug used as standard of care in GBM. It works by damaging DNA inside tumour cells, preventing them from replicating. Its effectiveness depends heavily on MGMT methylation status — in MGMT-methylated tumours, cells cannot repair the damage TMZ causes, making it significantly more effective.
→ Several elements of this protocol — CBD, metformin, sulforaphane, cannabinoids, melatonin — specifically enhance TMZ's effectiveness or overcome resistance to it. ↑ back
TRPV1
Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1
A receptor protein found on cell surfaces, originally known for its role in sensing heat and pain. In GBM, TRPV1 is overexpressed and serves as a target for CBD — activation of this receptor contributes to the apoptotic effect of CBD in tumour cells.
→ TRPV1 is one of the reasons CBD has anti-tumour activity through pathways entirely separate from the CB1/CB2 receptors that THC primarily targets — illustrating why the three cannabinoids together cover more ground than any single one alone. ↑ back
Warburg Effect
Named after Otto Warburg who first described it in the 1920s. Cancer cells — including GBM — preferentially burn glucose through a process called glycolysis even when oxygen is available, unlike normal cells. This inefficient but rapid energy generation supports fast tumour growth and creates a direct metabolic vulnerability: restrict glucose, and you restrict the tumour's primary fuel supply.
→ The Warburg Effect is the foundational scientific rationale for the entire metabolic strategy in this document — very low carb diet, intermittent fasting, metformin, exercise and MCT oil all target this vulnerability from different angles. ↑ back
Resveratrol
A polyphenol compound found naturally in red grapes, berries and wine. Has preclinical anti-tumour evidence but was removed from this protocol due to paradoxical pro-tumour effects seen at high doses in some cancer models, and its potential to raise NAD+ levels which may provide energy substrate to tumour cells.
→ A reminder that promising preclinical evidence is not always sufficient justification for inclusion — particularly when high-dose effects in cancer are not fully understood. Classification: Speculative. ↑ back
EGCG / Green Tea Extract
Epigallocatechin Gallate
The primary active compound in green tea, with genuine preclinical anti-tumour evidence. Excluded from this protocol due to significant CYP enzyme inhibition — affecting how other drugs are metabolised — and antiplatelet/anticoagulant properties creating bleeding risk in brain tumour patients on complex multi-drug protocols.
→ An important example of a supplement that sounds beneficial in isolation but creates unacceptable interaction risk in the context of a complex protocol. Do not add without direct oncologist supervision. ↑ back
NAD+
Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide
A coenzyme essential for cellular energy metabolism. NAD+ boosters (NMN, NR, resveratrol) have attracted interest for their anti-ageing properties — but in cancer, raising NAD+ may inadvertently fuel tumour cell energy production. This is why NMN/NR supplementation was also discontinued in this protocol.
→ Illustrates an important principle: interventions beneficial for general health are not always appropriate in an active cancer context. ↑ back
CYP Enzymes
Cytochrome P450 Enzymes
A family of liver enzymes responsible for metabolising the majority of drugs and many supplements. When a substance inhibits or induces these enzymes, it changes how other drugs are broken down — potentially raising or lowering their effective blood levels in unpredictable and dangerous ways.
→ Drug interactions via CYP enzymes are one of the main reasons complex multi-drug protocols require oncologist oversight. Green tea extract and quercetin at high doses are both notable CYP inhibitors worth monitoring. ↑ back
Buccal / Oromucosal Delivery
Administration of a drug via the mucous membranes of the mouth — either under the tongue (sublingual) or against the cheek (buccal). This route allows drugs to pass directly into the bloodstream without first passing through the liver — known as bypassing first-pass metabolism — resulting in more consistent and predictable drug levels.
→ Cannabinoids in this protocol are delivered via the buccal route for precisely this reason — it makes high-dose THC/CBD/CBG therapy more reliable and predictable than oral capsule delivery. ↑ back
Polyphenol
A large family of naturally occurring plant compounds characterised by multiple phenol rings in their chemical structure. Many have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-tumour properties. Curcumin, resveratrol, quercetin and EGCG (green tea extract) are all polyphenols.
→ Several polyphenols appear in this protocol's supplement stack. Their shared characteristic is poor oral bioavailability — most require specific formulations or co-factors (like fat, or black pepper for curcumin) to be absorbed effectively. ↑ back
Off-Label Prescribing
When a doctor prescribes a drug for a condition, patient group or dose that is outside its officially approved indications — but is supported by emerging evidence. Off-label prescribing is legal and common in oncology. Metformin for non-diabetic GBM patients is an example of off-label use.
→ Many of the repurposed drugs in this protocol require off-label prescribing. An oncologist willing to engage with this evidence is invaluable — building that relationship is itself a therapeutic act. ↑ back
Titration
The process of gradually adjusting a drug dose upward — starting low and increasing incrementally over time — until either the desired therapeutic effect is reached or the patient's tolerability ceiling is found. Used in medicine whenever a drug has significant dose-dependent effects that require the body to adapt progressively. The opposite of starting at full therapeutic dose immediately.
→ Titration is essential for high-dose THC therapy. Beginning at therapeutic dose (140mg) would be unsafe and likely intolerable. The 1:1:1 starting ratio scaling proportionally over weeks allows the body and brain to adapt while preserving the mechanistic balance of the three cannabinoids throughout. ↑ back
Cytostatic
A mechanism that stops cells from dividing and growing, without necessarily killing them. Different from apoptosis (which kills cells) — cytostatic agents effectively freeze tumour cells in place, preventing proliferation. Both mechanisms are valuable in cancer treatment and work in complementary ways.
→ CBG's primary mechanism against GBM is cytostatic — arresting the cell cycle at G1/S1 and G2/M checkpoints. This complements rather than duplicates the apoptotic pathways of THC and CBD. ↑ back
Equipoise
The ethical principle that a clinical trial is only justified when there is genuine uncertainty about which treatment arm is superior. If existing evidence already strongly favours one arm over another, the trial loses its ethical foundation — because randomising patients to the inferior arm causes avoidable harm. Equipoise is increasingly debated in GBM cannabinoid trials given existing Phase I data.
→ Understanding equipoise helps patients ask the right questions when offered trial participation — particularly in trials where placebo is one of the arms and prognosis is time-sensitive. ↑ back
Double-Blind Placebo-Controlled Trial
A trial design in which neither the patient nor the treating clinician knows which arm the patient has been randomised to — active treatment or placebo. Considered the gold standard for eliminating bias in clinical research. However, in trials involving psychoactive substances like THC, true blinding is practically impossible — patients receiving active THC will know from the effects, undermining the design's core assumption.
→ The ARISTOCRAT trial uses this design. Patients should understand what placebo randomisation means in the specific context of a terminal diagnosis with a time-sensitive treatment window — and make an informed personal decision about participation. ↑ back
PEMF Therapy
Pulsed Electromagnetic Field
A therapy that delivers low-frequency electromagnetic pulses to body tissue, proposed to support cellular repair, reduce inflammation and improve circulation. Has early preclinical evidence in neurological and oncological contexts. Not yet supported by Phase III trial data in GBM. Devices represent a significant upfront cost.
→ Classification: Speculative — preclinical and early-stage evidence only; no Phase II/III trial data in GBM. Worth awareness and discussion with your oncologist, but not included in the core protocol. ↑ back
Red Light Therapy
Photobiomodulation
The application of specific wavelengths of red and near-infrared light to tissue, proposed to stimulate mitochondrial function, reduce inflammation and support neurological recovery. Early-stage evidence is promising but not yet established in GBM-specific clinical trials.
→ Classification: Speculative — early-stage evidence only; no GBM-specific clinical trial data. Potentially useful for general recovery and inflammation reduction, but not established for GBM specifically. ↑ back
Radiation Necrosis
The death or damage of brain tissue caused by radiotherapy. Importantly, radiation necrosis does not stop at the end of treatment — the inflammatory process continues for months or years afterward. This can cause cognitive effects — memory issues, processing speed, fatigue — that emerge or worsen well after the radiotherapy that caused them has ended.
→ Understanding that cognitive effects may continue to develop post-treatment helps patients approach them proactively — with brain training, list-writing systems, and cognitive adaptation strategies — rather than being caught off guard. ↑ back
HRV
Heart Rate Variability
The variation in time between consecutive heartbeats. Counter-intuitively, higher HRV indicates better health — it means the nervous system is flexible and responsive. Lower HRV indicates physiological stress, poor recovery or immune activation. Measurable via smart rings and some smartwatches.
→ Daily HRV tracking provides an objective indicator of whether the body is recovered enough for exercise or needs rest — particularly valuable during chemotherapy cycles when recovery demand is higher. ↑ back
Konjac / Shirataki
A plant-derived fibre (glucomannan) used to make near-zero-carbohydrate pasta and rice substitutes. Konjac noodles and shirataki rice contain almost entirely indigestible fibre — they contribute negligible net carbohydrates while providing the texture and function of pasta or rice in a meal.
→ One of the most practically useful low-carb food substitutes available. Allows pasta and rice-based family meals to be shared without dietary compromise — a small but meaningful quality-of-life tool. ↑ back
Mindfulness
A practice of deliberately focusing attention on the present moment — noticing thoughts, sensations and surroundings without judgment. Evidence-based for reducing anxiety, improving sleep and supporting psychological resilience. Particularly valuable for patients who are not naturally optimistic, as it provides a practical route to psychological stability that does not require forced positivity.
→ Mindfulness and guided meditation (via apps like Calm or Headspace) are accessible, inexpensive and evidence-supported tools for managing the anxiety, night terrors and sleeplessness that commonly accompany a GBM diagnosis.
Electrolytes
Minerals dissolved in body fluids — primarily sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium and phosphate — that regulate nerve and muscle function, hydration and pH balance. During prolonged fasting, electrolyte levels can drop significantly as the kidneys excrete more minerals in the absence of food intake. Supplementation during extended fasts helps prevent cramps, fatigue, dizziness and cardiac irregularities.
→ Particularly important for GBM patients on TMZ during prolonged fasting, as TMZ already places demands on kidney function. Electrolyte supplementation during any fast beyond 24 hours should be discussed with your oncologist before proceeding. ↑ back
Essential Amino Acids (EAAs)
EAAs
The nine amino acids the body cannot synthesise itself and must obtain from diet or supplementation — histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Unlike full protein, EAA supplements provide the building blocks for muscle synthesis with minimal caloric load. During prolonged fasting, small doses of EAAs may preserve lean muscle mass without significantly raising insulin or breaking the ketotic state — though this remains an area of active research.
→ A key question to raise with your oncologist when considering prolonged fasting: whether EAA supplementation during the fast could protect lean mass — a clinical priority in GBM patients — without meaningfully compromising the metabolic GKI benefit. ↑ back
Cachexia
A complex metabolic syndrome associated with cancer and other serious illnesses, characterised by severe and progressive loss of muscle mass, fat, and body weight that cannot be fully reversed by nutritional support alone. Unlike ordinary weight loss, cachexia involves a fundamental disruption of normal metabolism driven by tumour-produced inflammatory signals. It affects up to 80% of cancer patients and independently worsens both treatment tolerance and survival outcomes.
→ Understanding cachexia explains why lean mass preservation is treated as a clinical priority throughout this protocol — not a cosmetic concern. High protein intake, resistance training, and careful monitoring of body composition are direct countermeasures to the cachectic processes that GBM treatment can trigger. ↑ back
Prolonged Fasting
Fasting periods extending beyond 24 hours — typically 3 to 7 days of water-only intake. Distinct from intermittent fasting in both depth of metabolic effect and physiological cost. Achieves GKI levels unattainable through diet alone, but carries meaningful risks of muscle loss and weight reduction that require clinical supervision.
→ A GBM case series achieved a mean GKI of 1.28 during prolonged fasting — compared to 5.10 on ketogenic diet alone. Compelling evidence, but timing around chemotherapy cycles and individual clinical context make oncologist consultation essential before attempting. ↑ back
Levetiracetam (Keppra)
An anti-epileptic drug and one of the most commonly prescribed seizure medications in GBM patients. Beyond seizure control, levetiracetam has a well-evidenced anti-tumour mechanism — it modulates HDAC enzymes, silencing MGMT and sensitising GBM cells to TMZ chemotherapy. A meta-analysis found a survival benefit specifically in unmethylated MGMT-promoter patients, suggesting it may partially compensate for the absence of natural methylation. Like lamotrigine, it avoids CYP enzyme induction that would compromise chemotherapy metabolism.
→ Patients prescribed levetiracetam for seizure control are simultaneously receiving a TMZ-sensitising agent — a dual benefit that is clinically underappreciated. Particularly relevant for unmethylated patients where every TMZ-sensitising mechanism carries additional weight. ↑ back
Clobazam
A benzodiazepine anti-epileptic used as a rescue or as-needed medication rather than a daily drug. Works by potentiating GABA-A receptors — raising the seizure threshold rapidly, typically within 20–30 minutes of administration. Used alongside a primary anti-epileptic (such as lamotrigine or levetiracetam) for situations where the seizure threshold is temporarily compromised by sleep deprivation, illness, stress, or disrupted routine.
→ Having clobazam prescribed and available transforms an elevated-risk situation from frightening to managed. Knowing exactly when and why to use it — agreed in advance with your clinical team — is itself a form of seizure prevention through anxiety reduction. ↑ back
Erythritol
A sugar alcohol used as a zero-calorie, zero-glycaemic sweetener. Unlike regular sugar, erythritol does not raise blood glucose or insulin levels — making it compatible with a very low carb protocol. It occurs naturally in small amounts in some fruits and fermented foods, and is produced commercially by fermenting glucose. Has a very similar taste and texture profile to sugar, making it the most effective substitute for baking and desserts.
→ The sweetener of choice in low carb baking, including Pete's wife's tiramisu recipe. Note that some people experience digestive sensitivity to large amounts of erythritol — start with small quantities to assess individual tolerance. ↑ back
GABA
Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid
The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system — essentially the brain's braking system. GABA reduces neuronal excitability, producing calming, anti-anxiety and anti-seizure effects. Benzodiazepines like clobazam work by enhancing GABA-A receptor activity, amplifying the brain's natural inhibitory system.
→ Understanding GABA helps explain why clobazam works quickly and effectively for both seizure risk and acute anxiety — it directly amplifies the brain's own calming mechanism rather than introducing a foreign signal. ↑ back
